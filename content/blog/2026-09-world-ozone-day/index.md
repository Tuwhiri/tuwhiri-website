The University of Otago marked World Ozone Day by asking Hannah Kessenich to
take apart some common myths about the ozone hole. She dispatched three of 
them in a single sentence.

[Read the full interview on the University website](https://www.otago.ac.nz/about/news/newsroom/dont-blame-the-ozone-hole-for-the-burn-and-other-facts)

## Three facts that would surprise most New Zealanders

**The hole is not above us.** It sits over Antarctica and the surrounding
ocean. Its edge grazes New Zealand occasionally, maybe one or two days a year, 
and that is all.

**It is not open year-round.** It opens for roughly three months each spring,
inside the polar vortex, and closes again.

**It is not why we burn.** New Zealanders face high UV because the Earth's
orbit brings the Southern Hemisphere slightly closer to the sun in our summer,
and because our air is unusually clean. Both would be true with no ozone hole
at all.

A fourth, less comfortable point: the hole is mostly a natural phenomenon.
Stratospheric winds and extreme cold over the pole account for the majority of it. 
Humans supplied the final ingredient by emitting chlorofluorocarbons, causing the hole
to open.

## Why this is Tuwhiri's problem too

The interview ends on something that will be familiar to anyone following this
programme. Asked about her research plans, Hannah mentions a paper in review
proposing a way to classify ozone hole severity — closely related to the
[diagnostic metric she presented at the EGU General Assembly in Vienna](/publications/kessenich-2026-a-novel-diagnostic-metric-for-quantifyin/)
in May — and then mentions, almost in passing, that she is helping build a new
instrument meant to take over from a NASA satellite.

That project is Tuwhiri.

Two of her observations explain why the instrument matters. The ozone hole has
been lasting longer in recent years than in any previous decade, and nobody yet
knows whether that is a signature of climate change. And current projections
have it continuing to open until around 2090 — projections which, she notes,
do not fully reproduce what is already being observed.

Both are questions you can only answer by continuing to observe the atmosphere. 
Aura's Microwave Limb Sounder, the instrument that has watched the Antarctic
stratosphere through the polar winter for two decades, will run out of fuel
with no replacement planned. Tuwhiri exists to build one small and cheap enough
that a country of New Zealand's size can afford to fly it.

## The other reason the hole matters

While the hole is open it creates a pocket of cold air over the South Pole 
that shifts circulation bands across the whole Southern Hemisphere — changing 
where winds strike New Zealand, how much rain they carry, and whether a summer 
brings drought or fire.

Hannah points to the October winds that brought trees down on the Otago campus
in 2025. This was in part the work of cold air being pushed north following an 
ozone hole destabilizing event. 

## How she got here

Hannah came to Otago from the United States following a physics degree and a job in
the technology sector. <q>I thought the ozone hole was old news and a solved problem. 
It turns out to have many unanswered questions.</q>, she says of the field. She went 
on to join under Annika Seppälä, who now leads Tuwhiri's atmospheric science. Those 
unanswered questions became her PhD. She completed it last year and is now a postdoctoral 
fellow in the Department of Physics.
